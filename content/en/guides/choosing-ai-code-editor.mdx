## The market has changed

A year ago, the question was whether to use an AI code editor at all. Now the question is which one. The market has fragmented into distinct design philosophies, and marketing copy makes them all sound identical: "AI-powered," "context-aware," "10x productivity."

This guide gives you a framework for cutting through that noise.

## Step 1: Understand the three design philosophies

AI code editors fall into three broad categories:

### AI-first editors

These editors were built around AI from the ground up. The AI model is not a plugin — it is the core editing experience. Cursor is the leading example. You get multi-file edits, repository-wide context, and AI chat anchored to specific code.

**Best for:** Teams that want maximum AI capability and will invest time in learning new workflows.

### AI-enhanced editors

These are traditional editors (usually VS Code) with an AI layer added on top. GitHub Copilot is the best-known example. You keep your existing editor, extensions, and settings; the AI supplements what you already know.

**Best for:** Developers who want AI help without changing their entire setup.

### Flow-first editors

These editors have AI built in but designed to stay quiet. Suggestions appear inline, context is narrower, and the philosophy is assistance without interruption. Windsurf represents this approach.

**Best for:** Developers who value focus and find aggressive AI suggestions distracting.

## Step 2: Define your non-negotiables

Before evaluating any specific tool, answer these questions:

1. **Can your code leave the machine?** If your organisation prohibits sending source code to external servers, this eliminates most cloud-dependent AI editors immediately.
2. **What is your budget?** Free tiers exist but have limits. Know what "free" actually means for each tool before assuming it will cover your usage.
3. **Do you work across many files or mostly in one?** Multi-file AI editing is a differentiator — but only if you actually need it.
4. **How important is editor speed?** Some AI editors use significantly more memory and CPU than their non-AI counterparts.

## Step 3: Run a real evaluation

Do not choose an AI code editor based on demos, YouTube videos, or feature lists. Here is how to evaluate honestly:

### The one-day test

Pick a real task from your backlog — not a toy project. Use the editor for one full working day. Track:

- **Setup time.** How long until you were productive?
- **Accepted suggestions.** What percentage of AI suggestions did you actually use?
- **Regressions.** Did the AI introduce bugs you had to fix?
- **Interruptions.** Did the AI break your flow, or enhance it?

### The team test

If your team is evaluating together, have at least two people use different editors on comparable tasks for a week. Compare notes on the same four metrics above.

## Step 4: Consider the exit cost

AI code editors create lock-in through configuration, custom workflows, and muscle memory. Before committing, ask:

- Can I export my settings if I switch?
- Are my files stored in standard formats?
- Does the editor depend on a proprietary backend that could change pricing?

## Common mistakes

1. **Choosing the most powerful tool.** More capability is not always better. An AI editor that interrupts your flow costs you more than the time it saves.
2. **Ignoring the free tier's actual limits.** "Free" often means limited completions, restricted models, or no multi-file editing. Test the free tier under real workload before assuming it is enough.
3. **Evaluating on demos.** Every AI editor looks impressive in a 3-minute video. Real code, on a real project, with real constraints, is the only honest test.
